The equivalent here was overdue. REQ-26 had nine days on it and no text behind
it, which is exactly what st-marys warned about earlier: on a deadline you draft
the deliverable before the decision comes back, not after. So there are now three
drafts in outputs - an RFQ to BSW to send by 6 August when their quotes lapse, an
RFQ to AFS by the 8th, and post-tender queries to Chigwell that can wait until
September.

They are split by clause 16 and each carries its reasoning at the head, so
whoever sends them knows why the tone differs. The supplier letters take what our
own terms make ours: measurement verification, supply of the agreed glazing
systems, and figures the supplier holds and we do not. The Chigwell letter takes
what the clause puts on the client's professional team, worded as questions and
reliances rather than defects.

Writing them is where the sort earned its keep. Agreeing with clause 16 in the
abstract is easy; drafting forces each finding into one document or the other, in
words. The biggest finding ended up in both, split - to BSW an instruction about
a product we buy, to Chigwell a question about a duty we do not set.

Two drafting choices worth keeping. Ask a supplier what they priced against
rather than why they got it wrong: the AFS door is quoted 100mm taller than the
opening, but our source is the same never-revised schedule that still shows a
deleted smoke shaft, so the letter says we accept the schedule may be wrong
rather than the quotation. And when a decision has been taken, say so in the
document: the Chigwell draft states we will honour the tendered figure per Adam's
ruling, and raises our own 30-day validity only to put it on record. A draft that
quietly reopens a decision its author accepted is worse than no draft.

Nothing is sent. A human issues all three.
